## Permission is mostly imaginary

**I spent way too much of my early university years waiting for permission to do things I wanted to do.**

Waiting for the "right time" to reach out to professors about research opportunities. Waiting to be "qualified enough" to apply for competitive internships. Waiting for someone to invite me to interesting events or communities.

**Here's what I wish I'd realized sooner:** Most of the barriers I thought existed were entirely in my head.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:lightbulb" classes="border-yellow-950/100 bg-yellow-100 dark:border-yellow-100/100 dark:bg-yellow-950">
  **The big insight:** You can just... do things. Send that email. Apply for that position. Start that project. Announce that crazy idea. The worst that happens is someone says no.
</Callout>

This isn't a "manifest your destiny" self-help post. This is a practical observation about how much opportunity exists in the space between having an idea and asking for permission to pursue it.

## The professor email I finally sent

**Let me start with a concrete example that changed how I think about this.**

In my third year, I became interested in machine learning applications in finance after taking a course on algorithmic trading. I kept reading papers by Dr. [Name], a professor in the computer science department who was doing exactly the kind of research I found fascinating.

For **six months**, I had her email drafted in my Gmail drafts folder. Six months of overthinking:

- *"Maybe I should wait until I've taken more ML courses"*
- *"I probably need to have a more specific research question"*
- *"She's probably too busy for undergraduate students"*
- *"I should get better grades first"*
- *"I should have more programming experience"*

**Classic overthinking spiral.** I was creating prerequisites that didn't actually exist, barriers that were entirely self-imposed.

Finally, a friend got tired of hearing me talk about this and basically shamed me into hitting send. The email was simple:

> Hi Dr. [Name],
> 
> I'm a third-year computer science student and I've been reading your recent papers on [specific research area]. I'm particularly interested in your work on [specific paper/project].
> 
> I'm wondering if you have any opportunities for undergraduate students to get involved in your research, either through volunteer work or research courses. I'd be happy to discuss my background and interests in more detail.
> 
> Best regards,
> [My name]

**She replied within 24 hours.** Not only did she have a project that would be perfect for an undergraduate research assistant, but she'd been looking for a student with exactly my background for weeks.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:mail">
  **The kicker:** She mentioned that very few undergraduates ever reach out directly. Most wait for formal research opportunity postings or go through intermediaries. By simply sending an email, I was already ahead of 90% of students who were just thinking about reaching out.
</Callout>

## The blog challenge that wasn't

**Another example:** Last month, I publicly announced I was going to do a "30 days of technical writing" challenge. Write one technical blog post every day for a month, share it on social media, build a habit around consistent content creation.

I made it 4 days.

**But here's the thing:** Those 4 posts were some of the best technical writing I'd ever done. The public commitment forced me to actually think through complex topics clearly enough to explain them to others. I got more engagement and feedback on those posts than on anything I'd written previously.

Did I "fail" the challenge? Sure, by my original metrics. But I got 80% of the value from 15% of the commitment. And more importantly, I learned that I could commit to ambitious public goals without having a perfect plan or being certain I could follow through.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:target" classes="border-blue-950/100 bg-blue-100 dark:border-blue-100/100 dark:bg-blue-950">
  **Plot twist:** The "failure" became its own valuable lesson. I wrote about why the challenge didn't work for me, what I learned from attempting it, and how I was adjusting my approach. That post got more attention than any of the original challenge posts.
</Callout>

## The opportunities hiding in plain sight

**Once you start looking for them, you realize opportunities are everywhere.** They're just disguised as things that require "permission" when they actually don't.

### Academic opportunities
- **Reaching out to professors about research** (seriously, just email them)
- **Proposing independent study courses** for topics you're interested in
- **Starting study groups** for difficult classes
- **Organizing reading groups** around interesting papers or books
- **Applying for conference travel grants** to present research (even preliminary work)

### Professional opportunities  
- **Cold-emailing professionals** you admire for informational interviews
- **Proposing projects** during internships instead of waiting for assignments
- **Starting meetups or discussion groups** around your professional interests
- **Contributing to open source projects** without being explicitly invited
- **Writing about your work** and sharing insights publicly

### Creative and personal opportunities
- **Starting that newsletter/blog/podcast** you've been thinking about
- **Organizing events** around your hobbies or interests
- **Teaching workshops** on skills you're good at
- **Building the tool** you wish existed
- **Connecting people** who should know each other

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:brain">
  **The common thread:** All of these require you to act first and ask for permission later (or never). They require you to believe that your ideas and interests are valid enough to pursue without external validation.
</Callout>

## Why we wait for permission (and why we shouldn't)

**There are legitimate reasons why we default to permission-seeking:**

### 1. Fear of rejection
Nobody likes being told no. But rejection in these contexts usually isn't personal—it's about timing, resources, or fit. And you're often not really losing anything you had before.

### 2. Imposter syndrome  
*"Who am I to reach out to this accomplished person?"* But here's the thing: most accomplished people were once students or beginners too. Many of them actively want to help others who remind them of their younger selves.

### 3. Perfectionist thinking
*"I need to be more prepared/qualified/experienced first."* But preparation without action is just elaborate procrastination. You'll learn more from one real conversation or project than from months of theoretical preparation.

### 4. Social conditioning
We're taught to follow rules, wait our turn, go through proper channels. These are good defaults for many situations, but they become limiting when applied universally.

**The reality check:** Most successful people got where they are by being proactive, not by waiting for opportunities to be handed to them.

## How to get better at just doing things

### 1. Start with low-stakes experiments

Don't begin with your most ambitious ideas. Practice with situations where the downside is minimal:

- Send an email asking a question you've been wondering about
- Suggest a meeting topic for a group you're part of
- Share an idea or resource with someone who might find it useful
- Comment thoughtfully on posts by people you find interesting

### 2. Reframe rejection as information

Every "no" tells you something useful:
- This person/organization isn't the right fit
- Your timing might be off
- You might need to adjust your approach
- You should try a different angle or person

**None of these indicate that you shouldn't have tried.**

### 3. Set implementation intentions

Instead of "I should reach out to that professor," try "I will send an email to Professor X on Tuesday at 2pm." Specific commitments are much more likely to happen than vague intentions.

### 4. Find accountability partners

Tell someone about your plans to take action. External accountability makes it harder to rationalize inaction.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:users">
  **Bonus:** Your accountability partner might have connections or ideas that help your project succeed. Other people are often more optimistic about your chances than you are.
</Callout>

## The compound effects of initiative

**Here's what happens when you get good at taking initiative:**

### Opportunities compound
Each action you take creates new possibilities. The professor introduces you to a graduate student working on something interesting. The professional you interview mentions a company that's hiring. The project you start leads to speaking opportunities.

### Reputation builds
People start to know you as someone who makes things happen. This makes them more likely to think of you when interesting opportunities arise.

### Confidence grows
Each time you take action and survive (regardless of the specific outcome), you prove to yourself that you can handle uncertainty and rejection. This makes bigger actions feel more achievable.

### Network effects
The more you put yourself out there, the more you encounter other people who also take initiative. These connections tend to be particularly valuable because they're action-oriented.

## What I'm doing differently now

**These days, I try to operate with a bias toward action rather than permission.**

When I have an idea for a project, I start it and see what happens rather than trying to plan every detail in advance.

When I meet someone interesting, I follow up within a few days with something useful—an article they might like, an introduction to someone they should meet, an invitation to continue the conversation.

When I see opportunities that seem slightly outside my "qualifications," I apply anyway and let them decide if I'm a fit.

**The key shift:** I stopped trying to be certain that something would work before starting it. Instead, I started treating uncertainty as information I could only gather through action.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:compass" classes="border-purple-950/100 bg-purple-100 dark:border-purple-100/100 dark:bg-purple-950">
  **Important caveat:** This approach requires good judgment about when rules actually matter and when they don't. Don't ignore legitimate requirements or disrespect people's time and boundaries. But don't create imaginary barriers either.
</Callout>

## The permission you've been waiting for

**If you're reading this and thinking about something you've been wanting to do but haven't started yet—this is your permission slip.**

That professor you want to contact? Send the email this week.

That blog you've been planning? Write the first post and hit publish.

That project idea you keep talking about? Start building a prototype.

That event you think should exist? Organize it.

That person you think you should meet? Reach out.

**You don't need to be perfectly qualified. You don't need a complete plan. You don't need anyone's permission.**

You just need to be curious, respectful, and willing to learn from whatever happens next.

### A few specific suggestions:

- **Research opportunities:** Browse your university's faculty pages and email professors whose work interests you
- **Professional connections:** Find people working in areas you're curious about and ask for 15-minute informational interviews
- **Creative projects:** Start that newsletter, blog, podcast, or YouTube channel you've been thinking about
- **Community building:** Organize a meetup, reading group, or study group around something you care about
- **Skill development:** Propose to teach a workshop on something you know, or find someone to teach you something you want to learn

## The worst that happens

**Let me be honest about the actual downsides:**

Sometimes people won't respond to your emails. Sometimes they'll say no. Sometimes your project won't work out the way you envisioned. Sometimes you'll feel foolish for trying.

**But here's what I've learned:** The regret of not trying is usually worse than the disappointment of trying and failing.

The projects that don't work out still teach you something. The emails that go unanswered don't actually harm you. The ideas that seem foolish in retrospect at least moved you forward from wherever you were stuck.

**The real risk isn't failure—it's spending years wondering "what if?"**

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:heart" classes="border-green-950/100 bg-green-100 dark:border-green-100/100 dark:bg-green-950">
  **Final thought:** Every interesting person I know has stories about opportunities that came from taking initiative, reaching out to strangers, or starting projects without permission. None of them regret trying too much. Many of them regret not trying sooner.
</Callout>

## Just start

**You already know what you want to try.** It's that thing you've been thinking about for weeks or months, the idea that keeps coming back to you in quiet moments.

Stop waiting for the perfect time, the perfect qualifications, or the perfect plan.

**You can just do things.**

So do them.
