## The networking truth that made me uncomfortable

**For most of my university career, I believed a comfortable lie: that in tech, merit matters more than relationships.**

I thought if I just got good enough at coding, learned the right frameworks, and built impressive projects, opportunities would naturally come my way. The best developers would rise to the top based purely on technical ability.

**Then I started paying attention to how people actually get jobs, promotions, and interesting projects.**

Almost every opportunity that came my way—internships, research positions, job offers, speaking opportunities—came through people I knew, not through cold applications or purely merit-based selections.

**The uncomfortable truth:** In tech, like everywhere else, relationships matter more than we want to admit.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:handshake">
  **The realization:** You can be the most technically skilled person in the room, but if no one knows who you are or what you're capable of, those skills won't translate into career advancement.
</Callout>

---

## Why I resisted networking

**Before I understood its importance, networking felt gross and inauthentic to me:**

### It seemed manipulative

The idea of building relationships for professional gain felt like using people. I wanted to believe that good work would speak for itself.

### It felt unfair

Networking seemed like it gave advantages to people who were naturally extroverted or socially skilled, rather than those who were technically excellent.

### It looked superficial

Watching people collect business cards at events and make small talk about the weather seemed like a waste of time compared to actually building things.

### It appeared transactional

The idea of reaching out to people only when I needed something felt selfish and dishonest.

### I thought I didn't need it

As a computer science student at a good university, I assumed my technical skills and academic credentials would be enough to open doors.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:brain" classes="border-orange-950/100 bg-orange-100 dark:border-orange-100/100 dark:bg-orange-950">
  **The mindset shift:** I eventually realized that networking doesn't have to be manipulative or transactional. At its best, it's about building genuine relationships with people who share your interests and can help each other grow.
</Callout>

## How opportunities actually work in tech

**Once I started paying attention, I noticed patterns in how people get good opportunities:**

### The hidden job market

Many positions are never posted publicly. Companies often hire people they know or people recommended by employees before creating job postings.

### Referrals carry enormous weight

Employee referrals get priority attention and are much more likely to result in interviews than cold applications, even with identical qualifications.

### Information advantages

People with good networks know about opportunities before they're public, can get inside information about what companies are really looking for, and can get advice on how to approach applications.

### Skill development through relationships

The best learning opportunities often come through connections: someone invites you to work on an interesting project, introduces you to a mentor, or suggests a technology you should explore.

### Reputation building

Your professional reputation spreads through relationships. People recommend you for opportunities based on their personal experience working with you, not just your resume.

### Access to interesting work

The most exciting projects and roles often go to people who are already in someone's network, not necessarily to the most qualified strangers.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:network">
  **The network effect:** Your career opportunities are largely determined by the opportunities that people in your network can access and are willing to share with you.
</Callout>

## What good networking actually looks like

**After years of doing it wrong, I learned that effective networking is different from what I imagined:**

### It's about genuine relationships, not transactions

The best professional relationships are built on mutual interest, shared values, and genuine liking for each other as people, not just professional utility.

### It's about giving, not just taking

The most valuable networkers are people who consistently help others—sharing opportunities, making introductions, offering advice—without expecting immediate returns.

### It's about long-term relationship building

Good networking is a long-term investment. You build relationships when you don't need anything, so they're there when opportunities arise.

### It's about shared interests and values

The strongest professional networks form around genuine shared interests: technical topics, industry problems, career goals, or even hobbies.

### It's about being authentically helpful

Instead of trying to "network" strategically, focus on being the kind of person others want to help and work with.

### It's about quality over quantity

Having deep relationships with a smaller number of people is much more valuable than superficial connections with hundreds of people.

## My networking evolution

### Phase 1: Accidental networking (first year)

I made friends with classmates and occasionally talked to professors after class. I didn't think of this as "networking"—it was just being social.

**What worked:** Being genuinely curious about what other people were working on and offering help when I could.

**What I missed:** I didn't maintain relationships with people after classes ended or when our paths diverged.

### Phase 2: Awkward strategic networking (second year)

I started attending "networking events" and trying to meet "important people" in tech. I felt awkward and inauthentic, and the connections I made were superficial.

**What didn't work:** Approaching networking as a strategic activity rather than genuine relationship building.

### Phase 3: Interest-based networking (third year)

I started attending meetups and events focused on topics I was genuinely interested in, not just career advancement. I met people who shared my technical interests.

**What worked:** Having genuine common ground made conversations natural and relationships sustainable.

### Phase 4: Giving-first networking (fourth year)

I started looking for ways to help other people: sharing job postings, making introductions, offering to review resumes, contributing to projects.

**What worked:** Being helpful to others made them want to help me in return, and it felt much more authentic.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:target" classes="border-green-950/100 bg-green-100 dark:border-green-100/100 dark:bg-green-950">
  **Key insight:** The transition from "What can I get from this person?" to "What can I offer this person?" was when networking started feeling natural and became genuinely effective.
</Callout>

## Specific strategies that worked for me

### Follow up on casual conversations

When someone mentions they're working on something interesting, I follow up with relevant articles, resources, or just questions about how the project is going.

### Make thoughtful introductions

When I meet two people who would benefit from knowing each other, I make introductions. This helps both people and strengthens my relationships with both.

### Share opportunities generously

When I see job postings, conference CFPs, or other opportunities that might interest people in my network, I share them—even if I'm not applying myself.

### Ask for advice, not jobs

Instead of asking people for job opportunities directly, I ask for advice about career paths, industry trends, or specific companies. This leads to much more helpful conversations.

### Maintain relationships during good times

I check in with people when I'm doing well and don't need anything, not just when I'm job hunting or looking for opportunities.

### Be genuinely interested in other people's work

I ask follow-up questions about projects people are working on, remember details from previous conversations, and celebrate their successes.

### Offer specific help

Instead of saying "let me know if I can help," I offer specific assistance: "I know someone at that company," "I can review your resume," "I'd be happy to practice interview questions with you."

## The coffee chat framework

**One of my most effective networking tools has been the informal coffee chat:**

### How to request them

> Hi [Name],
> 
> I really enjoyed your talk about [specific topic] at [event]. I'm a [your role] at [institution] and I'm particularly interested in [specific aspect of their work].
> 
> Would you have time for a brief coffee chat sometime in the next few weeks? I'd love to learn more about your experience with [specific topic] and get your advice on [specific question].
> 
> I'm happy to work around your schedule.
> 
> Thanks,
> [Your name]

### How to structure them

1. **Arrive prepared** with specific questions about their work, career path, or industry insights
2. **Listen more than you talk** and ask thoughtful follow-up questions
3. **Respect their time** by keeping it to the agreed duration (usually 30 minutes)
4. **Follow up** with a thank-you note and any resources or connections you promised

### What to ask about

- Their career path and major decisions
- Industry trends they're seeing
- Advice for someone in your position
- What they wish they had known earlier in their career
- Projects they're excited about
- People or resources they'd recommend

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:coffee">
  **Coffee chat etiquette:** Never ask for jobs directly in coffee chats. Focus on learning and building relationships. Opportunities will often emerge naturally from genuine connections.
</Callout>

## Online networking strategies

### Twitter/X engagement

Following industry leaders, commenting thoughtfully on their posts, and sharing interesting content has led to many valuable connections.

### LinkedIn content sharing

Writing posts about what I'm learning or working on has attracted connections from people with similar interests.

### GitHub contributions

Contributing to open source projects has led to relationships with maintainers and other contributors.

### Technical blogging

Writing about technical topics has led to conversations with other practitioners and occasionally speaking opportunities.

### Conference participation

Even when attending virtually, participating in Q&A sessions and connecting with speakers afterward has been valuable.

### Community involvement

Joining Discord servers, Slack communities, and forums related to my interests has led to ongoing relationships with people worldwide.

## What I got wrong about networking events

### Traditional networking events often don't work

Industry mixers and formal networking events can be effective for some people, but I found them awkward and superficial. The pressure to "network" made conversations feel forced.

### Interest-based events are much better

Meetups focused on specific technologies, industries, or topics led to much more natural conversations and stronger connections.

### The follow-up matters more than the event

The value of meeting someone isn't in the initial conversation—it's in the follow-up relationship you build afterward.

### Quality conversations beat quantity

Having one meaningful conversation is better than collecting twenty business cards from superficial interactions.

### Events are just the starting point

The real networking happens in the ongoing relationships you build after meeting someone, not in the initial meeting itself.

## The diversity and inclusion angle

**Networking advantages often compound existing privilege, which creates important equity considerations:**

### Not everyone has equal access to networks

People from underrepresented backgrounds may have fewer existing connections in tech and less access to informal networking opportunities.

### Cultural differences in networking

Different cultural backgrounds have different norms around professional relationship building, which can create advantages or disadvantages.

### Geographic and economic barriers

Networking events, conferences, and informal meetups often require time and money that not everyone can afford.

### The importance of inclusive networking

When you're in a position to help others, actively look for opportunities to support people who might have fewer traditional networking advantages.

### Using privilege responsibly

If you have networking advantages (through school, family connections, or other factors), consider how you can use them to help create opportunities for others.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:users" classes="border-blue-950/100 bg-blue-100 dark:border-blue-100/100 dark:bg-blue-950">
  **Important consideration:** Good networking should work to expand opportunities for everyone, not just reinforce existing advantages. Look for ways to make your own network more diverse and inclusive.
</Callout>

## Common networking mistakes to avoid

### Being transactional

Only reaching out to people when you need something makes you seem selfish and damages long-term relationships.

### Focusing only on "important" people

Some of the most valuable connections I've made have been with peers rather than senior industry leaders. Don't overlook people at your own level.

### Not following up

Meeting someone and then never staying in touch wastes the initial investment of building rapport.

### Making it all about you

Dominating conversations with your own projects and achievements rather than showing interest in others.

### Being too aggressive

Pushing too hard for immediate opportunities rather than building genuine relationships first.

### Neglecting existing relationships

Constantly seeking new connections while ignoring the relationships you already have.

### Over-promising and under-delivering

Offering help that you can't or don't actually provide damages your reputation and relationships.

## How networking changes your career

**Building a strong professional network has tangible career benefits:**

### Better job opportunities

Most of my best job opportunities have come through network connections rather than public job boards.

### Faster learning

Having access to experienced practitioners who can answer questions, provide guidance, and share resources accelerates professional development.

### Industry insights

Understanding industry trends, company cultures, and career paths through insider perspectives rather than public information.

### Collaboration opportunities

Getting invited to work on interesting projects, speak at events, or contribute to initiatives outside your main job.

### Career guidance

Having mentors and advisors who can provide career advice based on their own experience and industry knowledge.

### Professional reputation

Building a reputation within your professional community that leads to opportunities and recognition.

### Resilience during career transitions

Having a network to lean on during job searches, career changes, or difficult professional situations.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:trending-up">
  **Career acceleration:** A strong network doesn't just help you get jobs—it helps you get better jobs, more interesting projects, and faster career progression than would be possible through technical skills alone.
</Callout>

## Advice for introverted networkers

**As someone who's naturally introverted, I've learned strategies that make networking more sustainable:**

### Focus on one-on-one conversations

Large group events are draining, but individual coffee chats are much more manageable and often more valuable.

### Prepare conversation starters

Having a few questions ready reduces the anxiety of not knowing what to talk about.

### Set realistic goals

Aim to have one meaningful conversation rather than meeting as many people as possible.

### Use your listening skills

Introverts are often good listeners, which is actually a networking superpower. People appreciate being heard.

### Follow up in writing

Email or message follow-ups play to introverts' strengths and give you time to craft thoughtful responses.

### Find smaller communities

Intimate meetups or online communities can be less overwhelming than large conferences.

### Network around your interests

It's easier to talk to people when you're genuinely interested in the topic of conversation.

## The long-term view

**Good networking is a long-term investment that compounds over time:**

### Relationships deepen

People you meet as students or junior employees become senior leaders who can provide opportunities and guidance.

### Networks expand

Your connections introduce you to their connections, exponentially expanding your professional reach.

### Reputation builds

Consistently being helpful and reliable builds a reputation that opens doors you didn't even know existed.

### Opportunities multiply

Each good opportunity often leads to more opportunities through the relationships you build in that role.

### Career resilience increases

A strong network provides support and opportunities during career transitions, industry changes, or economic downturns.

## What I wish I'd known earlier

**If I could go back and give my younger self networking advice:**

1. **Start building relationships before you need them**
2. **Be genuinely helpful to others without expecting immediate returns**
3. **Quality relationships matter more than quantity of connections**
4. **Follow up and stay in touch—relationships require maintenance**
5. **Don't underestimate the value of peer relationships**
6. **Networking is about shared interests, not just career advancement**
7. **Your reputation is built through consistent small actions over time**
8. **Everyone has something valuable to offer, regardless of their position**
9. **Online relationships can be just as valuable as in-person ones**
10. **The goal is mutual benefit, not one-sided advantage**

**The most important lesson:** Networking isn't about using people—it's about building a professional community where everyone helps each other succeed.
