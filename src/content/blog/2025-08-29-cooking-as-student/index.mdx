## From ramen to real food

**In my first year of university, my cooking skills were limited to:**
- Instant ramen (various flavors)
- Microwave mac and cheese
- Cereal (technically not cooking)
- Toast (if I'm being generous)
- Takeout ordering (my specialty)

**I was the stereotypical student who lived on processed food, spent way too much money on delivery apps, and felt tired and sluggish most of the time.** I knew I should learn to cook, but it seemed overwhelming, time-consuming, and honestly kind of boring.

**Fast forward to fourth year:** I genuinely enjoy cooking, prepare most of my meals at home, have significantly more energy, and spend way less money on food. Learning to cook has been one of the most practically valuable skills I've developed during university.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:lightbulb" classes="border-yellow-950/100 bg-yellow-100 dark:border-yellow-100/100 dark:bg-yellow-950">
  **The transformation:** Cooking went from being a chore I avoided to being a relaxing, creative activity that saves me money, improves my health, and gives me a sense of accomplishment.
</Callout>

## Why I initially avoided cooking

### It seemed too complicated

**Looking at recipes with 15 ingredients and complex techniques** made cooking seem like something that required culinary school training, not something a busy student could manage.

### Time pressure

**Between classes, assignments, and social commitments,** spending an hour cooking dinner seemed like an inefficient use of time when I could order food in 10 minutes.

### Fear of waste

**I was worried about buying ingredients and having them go bad** before I could use them, which felt financially irresponsible as a student.

### Lack of equipment

**My residence kitchen was basic,** and I assumed I needed expensive equipment and a full spice collection to cook anything decent.

### No apparent reward

**The food I attempted to make was usually worse than what I could buy,** so there didn't seem to be any benefit to the extra effort.

### Social aspects

**Eating out or ordering in was often social** - studying with friends over takeout, trying new restaurants. Cooking alone felt isolating by comparison.

---

## The breaking point

**What finally motivated me to learn was a combination of factors in my third year:**

### Budget crisis

**I calculated that I was spending over $400 per month on food** - mostly delivery and dining out. As a student, this was unsustainable and was preventing me from doing other things I wanted to do.

### Energy problems

**I felt constantly tired and sluggish.** My diet of processed food, sugar, and caffeine was creating energy crashes that affected my academic performance and mood.

### Living situation change

**Moving into an apartment with a proper kitchen** made cooking more practical and appealing than it had been in residence.

### Health wake-up call

**A routine checkup revealed that my blood pressure and cholesterol were higher than they should be for someone my age.** My doctor suggested dietary changes as a first step.

### Stress from poor nutrition

**I started noticing that my mood and stress levels were closely tied to what I ate.** Junk food made me feel anxious and unfocused, which made studying harder.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:alert-triangle">
  **The realization:** My food choices weren't just affecting my budget—they were impacting my academic performance, physical health, mental wellbeing, and overall quality of life.
</Callout>

## My gradual approach to learning

### Phase 1: Master the basics (Month 1-2)

Instead of trying to cook elaborate meals, I focused on getting comfortable with fundamental techniques:

**Basic skills I practiced:**
- Boiling pasta properly (timing, salting water, not overcooking)
- Scrambling eggs (low heat, patience, constant stirring)
- Sautéing vegetables (proper oil temperature, not overcrowding the pan)
- Cooking rice (ratios, timing, not lifting the lid)
- Basic knife skills (how to hold a knife, basic cuts)

**Simple meals I mastered:**
- Pasta with various simple sauces
- Scrambled eggs with vegetables
- Stir-fried rice with whatever ingredients I had
- Basic salads with homemade dressing
- Baked chicken with roasted vegetables

### Phase 2: Build a foundation (Month 3-4)

Once I was comfortable with basics, I focused on building a repertoire of reliable meals:

**Core recipes I learned:**
- A few different pasta sauces (marinara, aglio e olio, carbonara)
- Basic curry that I could customize with different proteins and vegetables
- Simple soups (chicken noodle, vegetable, lentil)
- Sheet pan meals (protein + vegetables roasted together)
- Basic breakfast options beyond cereal

**Kitchen equipment I gradually acquired:**
- Good knife (made everything easier and safer)
- Cutting board large enough to work on
- Non-stick pan (forgiving for beginners)
- Sheet pan (versatile for roasting)
- Basic spices and seasonings

### Phase 3: Develop personal preferences (Month 5-6)

**I started paying attention to what flavors I enjoyed** and learning to cook foods I actually wanted to eat rather than just following random recipes.

**Discoveries about my preferences:**
- I prefer fresh herbs over dried when possible
- I like meals with some textural variety (crunchy elements in soft dishes)
- I enjoy spicy food more than I thought
- I prefer simple preparations that highlight good ingredients
- I'm more likely to cook if prep work is minimal

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:target" classes="border-green-950/100 bg-green-100 dark:border-green-100/100 dark:bg-green-950">
  **Key insight:** Starting with simple, reliable recipes built my confidence and helped me understand basic principles before attempting more complex dishes.
</Callout>

## Strategies that made learning sustainable

### Meal planning and batch cooking

**Instead of deciding what to cook each day,** I started planning meals for the week and preparing components in advance:

- **Sunday prep:** Washing and chopping vegetables, cooking grains, preparing proteins
- **Batch cooking:** Making large portions of soups, curries, or sauces that could be used for multiple meals
- **Strategic leftovers:** Cooking extra protein that could be used in different ways throughout the week

### The "good enough" philosophy

**I gave myself permission to make imperfect food** rather than striving for restaurant quality from the beginning. Slightly overcooked vegetables were still better than no vegetables.

### Start with cuisines you already like

**Instead of trying to cook everything,** I focused on learning to make versions of foods I already enjoyed ordering: pasta, stir-fries, simple curries, roasted meats and vegetables.

### One new thing per week

**Rather than overwhelming myself with completely new recipes,** I would try one new technique or ingredient each week while relying on familiar preparations for other meals.

### Use technology strategically

**Cooking apps and YouTube videos** were incredibly helpful for learning techniques and timing. I could watch someone demonstrate proper knife skills or pasta cooking techniques.

### Accept that some meals will fail

**I planned for disasters** by keeping backup options (frozen meals, easy pasta dishes) for when experiments went wrong.

## Budget-friendly strategies for student cooking

### Smart shopping habits

- **Shop sales and seasonal produce** - plan meals around what's affordable that week
- **Buy versatile ingredients** - items that can be used in multiple different meals
- **Bulk bins for spices** - much cheaper than packaged spices
- **Generic brands** - often 30-50% cheaper with minimal quality difference
- **Frozen vegetables** - often more nutritious than fresh and always available

### Ingredient maximization

- **One protein, multiple preparations** - buy chicken in bulk, use it in pasta, stir-fry, soup, sandwiches
- **Base ingredients that stretch** - rice, pasta, potatoes, beans can form the foundation of many different meals
- **Leftover transformation** - roasted vegetables become soup, extra rice becomes fried rice

### Equipment priorities

**Focus on a few versatile, quality items rather than lots of specialized tools:**
- Good knife (worth investing in)
- Large non-stick pan
- Sheet pan
- Large pot for pasta/soup
- Cutting board
- Basic measuring tools

### Meal planning to prevent waste

- **Plan before shopping** - know what you're going to make before buying ingredients
- **Shop your pantry first** - use what you have before buying new ingredients
- **Flexible recipes** - choose dishes that can adapt to whatever vegetables or proteins are on sale

## Simple recipes that got me started

### Basic pasta aglio e olio

**Ingredients:** Pasta, garlic, olive oil, red pepper flakes, parmesan
**Why it works:** Few ingredients, hard to mess up, teaches basic techniques

### Sheet pan chicken and vegetables

**Ingredients:** Chicken thighs, whatever vegetables you have, olive oil, salt, pepper
**Why it works:** One pan, minimal prep, hard to overcook

### Basic fried rice

**Ingredients:** Leftover rice, eggs, vegetables, soy sauce, oil
**Why it works:** Uses leftovers, very forgiving, endlessly customizable

### Simple curry

**Ingredients:** Protein, vegetables, canned tomatoes, curry powder, coconut milk
**Why it works:** One pot, makes lots of servings, improves with time

### Basic salad with homemade dressing

**Ingredients:** Greens, whatever vegetables you have, oil, vinegar, mustard
**Why it works:** No cooking required, teaches balance of flavors

## Common beginner mistakes I made

### Trying recipes that were too complex

**Starting with elaborate dishes** led to frustration and failure. Simple recipes build confidence and teach fundamental skills.

### Not reading the entire recipe first

**Starting to cook without understanding all the steps** led to timing issues and missing ingredients.

### Using too high heat

**Most things cook better at medium or medium-low heat.** High heat usually just burns food without cooking it properly.

### Not tasting as I cooked

**Seasoning and adjusting flavors throughout the cooking process** is essential for good results.

### Buying too many ingredients at once

**Overambitious grocery shopping** led to waste when I couldn't use everything before it spoiled.

### Comparing my food to restaurant meals

**Expecting restaurant-quality results immediately** was unrealistic and discouraging. Home cooking has different goals and constraints.

## Advice for student cooking beginners

### Start embarrassingly simple

Don't worry about impressing anyone. Master scrambled eggs before attempting beef wellington.

### Focus on techniques, not recipes

Learn how to sauté, roast, and boil properly. These techniques apply to countless different dishes.

### Cook what you like to eat

You'll be more motivated to cook foods you actually want to eat rather than foods you think you should eat.

### Plan for failure

Keep backup meal options for when experiments don't work out. Don't let one bad meal discourage you.

### Use your resources

YouTube, cooking apps, and friends who cook are all valuable sources of knowledge and encouragement.

### Make it social when possible

Cook with friends, for friends, or while video chatting with family. It's more enjoyable and you'll learn faster.

### Invest in one good knife

A sharp, comfortable knife makes everything easier and safer. It's worth spending money on.
