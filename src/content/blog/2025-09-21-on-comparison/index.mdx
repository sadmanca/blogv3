## The newsletter that made me feel inadequate

**There's this one fellow student whose newsletter I read.**

I started following it because I thought he was really accomplished—smart guy, doing interesting things, seemed to have his life figured out in ways I definitely don't. So when I saw he had a newsletter, I thought, "Sure, let me read more about this smart person's thoughts."

**And wow, does this person do so much.**

Every week, there's a new update about some project he's working on, some opportunity he's pursuing, some skill he's developing, some network he's building. Internships at prestigious companies, side projects getting featured in major publications, speaking at conferences, building startups, mentoring other students—the list goes on and on.

Reading his updates makes me feel... well, kinda inadequate.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:scale">
  **Here's the thing about comparison:** It's almost always unfair to yourself. You're comparing your internal experience (full of doubt, struggle, and ordinary moments) to someone else's highlight reel.
</Callout>

---


## The cult of constant productivity

**We live in a culture that glorifies the hustle.**

Every social media feed is full of people sharing their morning routines at 5 AM, their side projects, their networking events, their latest achievements. There's this implicit message that if you're not constantly optimizing, constantly building, constantly growing, you're somehow falling behind.

**But that's bullshit.**

Rest isn't the enemy of progress. Taking breaks isn't laziness. Having days where you don't accomplish everything on your list isn't failure.

### The things we forget when we're hustling too hard:

- **Your brain needs downtime** to process information and form connections
- **Your body needs recovery** to actually build strength and maintain health  
- **Your relationships need attention** that isn't divided between twelve other priorities
- **Your mental health needs space** to breathe and just exist without constantly striving

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:brain">
  **Research actually supports this:** Studies consistently show that people who take regular breaks, get adequate sleep, and have periods of low stimulation are more creative, more productive, and healthier in the long run.
</Callout>

## The fellow student's newsletter (revisited)

**Going back to that newsletter that made me feel inadequate—I actually still read it.**

But now I read it differently. Instead of using it as a measuring stick for my own life, I appreciate it for what it is: one person's journey, shared openly. 

Maybe he's naturally higher energy than me. Maybe he's in a different phase of life. Maybe he has different priorities, different circumstances, different support systems. Maybe he's also struggling with things he doesn't share in his newsletter.

**The point isn't that I should be doing less or more—it's that I should be doing what works for me.**

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:users">
  **Here's what I wish I could tell my past self:** Other people's achievements aren't indictments of your own progress. There's enough success to go around. Someone else doing well doesn't mean you're doing poorly.
</Callout>

## What I'm learning about balance

**These days, I'm trying to be more intentional about rest and recovery.**

Not just physical recovery (though that's important too), but mental and emotional recovery. Time when I'm not optimizing anything, not working toward any particular goal, just existing and enjoying existing.

**Some things that have helped:**

### 1. Reframing rest as productive
Rest isn't the absence of productivity—it's a different kind of productivity. You're producing recovery, mental clarity, and long-term sustainability.

### 2. Planning for downtime
If I don't actively plan for rest, it doesn't happen. I'll fill every available moment with tasks and projects. So I literally block out time for doing nothing.

### 3. Celebrating small wins
Instead of only acknowledging major achievements, I try to notice and appreciate smaller things: a good conversation, a nice meal, a moment of calm, a problem solved, a workout completed.

### 4. Remembering that seasons change
Sometimes you're in a building season, sometimes you're in a resting season, sometimes you're in a learning season. Fighting against the natural rhythm usually makes things harder.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:calendar" classes="border-blue-950/100 bg-blue-100 dark:border-blue-100/100 dark:bg-blue-950">
  **Important insight:** Your energy and capacity aren't constant. They fluctuate based on sleep, stress, health, seasons, life circumstances, and a dozen other factors. Working with these fluctuations instead of against them is much more sustainable.
</Callout>

## The minimum viable effort

**Here's something I've started asking myself:** What's the minimum viable effort I can put in today and still feel good about it?

Some days, that's crushing a big project and having energy left over for a workout and social plans. Other days, it's answering a few emails and taking a shower.

**Both are valid. Both are enough.**

The goal isn't to optimize every single day—it's to show up consistently over time in a way that doesn't burn you out or make you hate the things you care about.

---

## A note to the overthinkers

**If you're someone who tends to overthink productivity and self-improvement (hi, me too), here's something worth considering:**

The time you spend feeling guilty about not doing enough is time you could be either (a) actually doing something, or (b) genuinely resting and recovering so you can do better later.

**Guilt is not a useful middle ground.**

Either commit to working on something, or commit to taking a break. Both are fine choices. The anxious in-between state where you're neither working effectively nor resting effectively is what really drains your energy.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:zap">
  **The productivity paradox:** Sometimes the most productive thing you can do is stop trying to be productive and just let yourself be human for a while.
</Callout>

---

## What actually matters

**At the end of the day—or at the end of a life—what matters?**

I don't think it's the number of side projects you completed or the networking events you attended or the optimization systems you built. I think it's whether you showed up as yourself, whether you were kind to people (including yourself), whether you found ways to enjoy the ordinary moments, and whether you built something meaningful over time.

**All of that requires periods of rest, reflection, and just being.**

The world will not end if you take a day off. Your career will not be derailed if you skip one networking event. Your fitness goals will not be ruined if you miss one workout.

**But your mental health might suffer if you never give yourself permission to be imperfect, to be tired, to be human.**
