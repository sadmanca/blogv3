## The internship I thought I wanted

**When I landed my first remote internship, I was thrilled.**

No commute, flexible hours, the ability to work from anywhere, and all the supposed benefits of remote work that everyone talks about. Plus, it was with a company I really wanted to work for, doing the kind of technical work I was excited to learn.

**For the first week, it felt amazing.** I could wake up later, work in my pajamas, take breaks whenever I wanted, and avoid all the office politics and distractions that come with in-person work.

**But by the third week, something was wrong.** I felt isolated, unmotivated, and like I wasn't really learning as much as I should be. I was technically getting my work done, but I felt disconnected from the company, my team, and even the work itself.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:users">
  **The thing no one warns you about:** Remote internships can be incredibly isolating, especially when you're young, new to the industry, and trying to learn not just technical skills but also workplace culture and professional relationships.
</Callout>

---

## What makes remote internships different

**Remote internships aren't just regular internships that happen to be done from home. They're fundamentally different experiences with unique challenges:**

### The learning curve is steeper

When you're in an office, you absorb information constantly through osmosis. You overhear conversations about architecture decisions, you see how people interact with different stakeholders, you witness how problems get solved in real time.

Working remotely, you only get information that's explicitly shared with you. There's no casual learning, no accidental insights, no picking up context from the environment around you.

### Relationship building is much harder

Some of my most valuable professional relationships started with casual conversations in hallways, shared coffee breaks, or just sitting near someone interesting. Remote work eliminates these serendipitous interactions.

Building relationships becomes a much more intentional, formal process—which is difficult when you're an intern who doesn't yet know who you should be building relationships with or how to approach people.

### The feedback loop is slower

When you're physically present, you can read body language, catch quick corrections, and get immediate feedback on your approach to problems. Remote work often means working for hours or days before getting input on whether you're heading in the right direction.

### Imposter syndrome is amplified

It's easier to feel like you don't belong when you're sitting alone in your room rather than surrounded by other people doing similar work. You start to wonder if everyone else is more productive, more engaged, or just better at this whole "professional" thing.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:brain" classes="border-orange-950/100 bg-orange-100 dark:border-orange-100/100 dark:bg-orange-950">
  **The isolation paradox:** You're technically more connected than ever (video calls, Slack, email), but you can feel profoundly disconnected from your work, your team, and your own professional development.
</Callout>

## The daily reality of remote internship life

**Let me walk you through what a typical day looked like during my remote internship:**

### 9:00 AM: Wake up and check Slack

Roll out of bed, grab coffee, and scan through messages that came in overnight. Sometimes there are things that need immediate attention, sometimes not. Either way, you're already in work mode before you've even had breakfast.

### 9:30 AM: Daily standup call

Sit in your pajamas on a video call with your team, giving updates on yesterday's work and today's plans. You try to sound engaged and professional while sitting in the same room where you sleep, eat, and relax.

### 10:00 AM - 12:00 PM: Deep work time

This is usually the most productive part of the day. You're fresh, there aren't many meetings yet, and you can focus on coding or whatever your main tasks are. This part actually works pretty well remotely.

### 12:00 PM - 1:00 PM: Lunch break (alone)

Make a sandwich and eat at your desk while browsing Reddit or watching YouTube. Sometimes you eat lunch during a meeting because it's easy to forget to take breaks when your workspace is your living space.

### 1:00 PM - 4:00 PM: Meetings and collaborative work

Video calls, code reviews, planning sessions. This is where the limitations of remote work become apparent. Conversations take longer, it's harder to brainstorm collaboratively, and technical discussions over video can be frustrating.

### 4:00 PM - 6:00 PM: Finishing up tasks

Working on follow-ups from meetings, trying to wrap up the day's work. But when your office is your bedroom, it's hard to have a clean end to the workday.

### 6:00 PM+: "End" of workday

Close your laptop, but you're still in the same physical space. No commute to decompress, no physical separation between work and personal time. Often you end up checking Slack again before bed "just to see if anything urgent came up."

**The result:** Days blend together, you miss the natural rhythms and social interactions that make work feel meaningful, and you start to question whether you're actually learning what you need to learn.

## What you miss without office culture

### The unofficial curriculum

In an office environment, there's an unofficial curriculum that happens through casual interactions:

- **Overhearing technical discussions** that teach you how experienced developers think through problems
- **Seeing how different people approach similar challenges** and learning from their different styles
- **Understanding office dynamics** and how decisions actually get made (not just how they're supposed to get made)
- **Learning the unwritten rules** of professional communication and behavior

### Mentorship opportunities

Some of the best mentorship happens spontaneously when someone notices you struggling with something and offers help, or when you see how someone approaches a problem and ask follow-up questions.

Remote work makes these interactions much less likely. Mentorship becomes something that has to be formally arranged rather than naturally occurring.

### Social capital building

Building social capital—the network of relationships and reputation that helps your career—is much harder when your interactions are limited to formal meetings and Slack messages.

You miss out on the casual conversations that help people get to know you as a person, not just as someone who delivers work.

### Company culture immersion

Company culture is hard to absorb through video calls and documentation. The real culture exists in how people interact when they think no one is watching, how they handle stress, what they actually prioritize versus what they say they prioritize.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:building">
  **The culture gap:** You can learn a company's stated values from their website, but you learn their actual values from spending time around their people. Remote internships make it much harder to understand what a company is really like to work for.
</Callout>

## The productivity illusion

**One of the most confusing things about my remote internship was that I seemed to be productive, but I didn't feel like I was learning as much as I should be.**

### Task completion vs. skill development

I was completing assigned tasks and getting positive feedback, but I wasn't developing the broader skills and intuition that come from being immersed in a professional environment.

I could write code and solve specific problems, but I wasn't learning how to navigate ambiguous requirements, how to communicate technical concepts to non-technical stakeholders, or how to build consensus around design decisions.

### Individual work vs. collaborative skills

Most of my work could be done independently, which felt efficient but didn't teach me the collaborative skills that are essential in most professional environments.

I missed out on learning how to contribute effectively to group discussions, how to give and receive code reviews gracefully, and how to work through disagreements constructively.

### Short-term delivery vs. long-term thinking

Remote work often emphasizes individual deliverables and short-term goals because those are easier to measure and manage. But this can miss the bigger picture thinking and strategic understanding that are crucial for career development.

### The feedback desert

Getting feedback on your work is different from getting feedback on your professional development. I received plenty of comments on my code and specific deliverables, but very little guidance on how I was developing as a professional.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:target" classes="border-green-950/100 bg-green-100 dark:border-green-100/100 dark:bg-green-950">
  **Key insight:** Productivity metrics don't capture the full value of an internship. Learning to work effectively with others, understanding business context, and developing professional judgment are harder to measure but often more valuable than task completion.
</Callout>

## The social isolation factor

### Working alone all day

Humans are social creatures, and work provides a significant portion of social interaction for most people. When you're working alone all day, even with video calls, you miss out on the casual social interaction that makes work feel connected to human relationships.

### Missing peer connections

Some of the most valuable relationships you build during internships are with other interns or junior employees who are going through similar experiences. Remote work makes these peer connections much harder to develop.

### The small talk deficit

It sounds trivial, but small talk serves important functions: it builds rapport, provides context about people's lives and motivations, and creates shared experiences that strengthen working relationships.

Without casual interactions, professional relationships can feel transactional rather than personal.

### Loneliness amplification

If you're already prone to loneliness or social anxiety, remote work can amplify these feelings. You spend most of your day in isolation, and when you do interact with people, it's in formal, work-focused contexts.

## Coping strategies that helped

**Despite these challenges, I did develop some strategies that made the experience more bearable and educational:**

### Proactive communication

I learned to over-communicate rather than under-communicate. Instead of waiting for people to check in with me, I regularly updated my manager on progress, challenges, and questions.

I also made an effort to ask clarifying questions even when I thought I understood something, because the cost of being wrong was higher when feedback loops were slower.

### Intentional relationship building

I scheduled informal coffee chats with team members, asked if I could observe meetings even when my participation wasn't required, and volunteered for collaborative projects even when I could have worked alone.

### Creating structure and boundaries

I established clear work hours and physical boundaries to maintain some separation between work and personal life. This included getting dressed for work, using a dedicated workspace, and having a routine to "commute" from work mode to personal mode.

### Seeking multiple perspectives

Since I wasn't getting diverse viewpoints through casual interactions, I made an effort to seek out different perspectives by talking to people in different roles and departments.

### Documenting learning

I kept a journal of what I was learning—not just technical skills, but also observations about how the company worked, what I was struggling with, and what I wanted to focus on next.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:lightbulb">
  **The extra effort required:** All of these strategies required much more intentional effort than they would have in an in-person environment. Remote internships demand more proactive self-advocacy and relationship management.
</Callout>

## What companies could do better

**Based on my experience, here are some ways companies could make remote internships more effective:**

### Structured onboarding for remote culture

Don't just adapt in-person onboarding for video calls. Design onboarding specifically for remote work that addresses the unique challenges of building relationships and understanding company culture from a distance.

### Intentional mentorship programs

Pair interns with mentors who understand the challenges of remote work and are committed to regular, meaningful check-ins that go beyond task management.

### Virtual co-working sessions

Create opportunities for informal interaction through virtual co-working sessions, online coffee chats, or informal Slack channels where people can share non-work conversation.

### Rotation opportunities

Give interns exposure to different teams, projects, or aspects of the business to compensate for the reduced serendipitous learning that happens in office environments.

### Clear career development focus

Be more explicit about career development goals and regularly discuss not just what interns are doing, but what they're learning and how it fits into their longer-term professional development.

### Regular feedback and check-ins

Increase the frequency of feedback and make it more comprehensive, covering not just work quality but also professional development, company integration, and personal wellbeing.

## The hybrid alternative

**If I were to do an internship again, I think I'd advocate for a hybrid approach rather than fully remote:**

### Best of both worlds

Coming into the office 2-3 days per week provides opportunities for the casual interactions and relationship building that are hard to replicate remotely, while still offering the flexibility and focus time that remote work provides.

### Intentional in-person time

When you are in the office, you can be more intentional about using that time for collaborative work, relationship building, and absorbing company culture.

### Better work-life separation

Having some physical separation between work and home helps maintain boundaries and prevents the work-from-bedroom burnout that many remote workers experience.

### Team building opportunities

Hybrid schedules allow for intentional team building activities, group problem-solving sessions, and social interactions that strengthen working relationships.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:compass" classes="border-blue-950/100 bg-blue-100 dark:border-blue-100/100 dark:bg-blue-950">
  **The balance:** Different people have different needs, but for most interns, some in-person interaction is valuable for learning, relationship building, and professional development.
</Callout>

## Long-term career implications

**The isolation and learning gaps from remote internships can have longer-term career implications:**

### Networking deficits

The professional relationships you don't build during internships can affect your access to future opportunities, mentorship, and career advice.

### Skill development gaps

Missing out on collaborative skills, office dynamics, and cultural understanding can make the transition to full-time work more difficult.

### Industry understanding

Without exposure to how companies actually operate day-to-day, it's harder to make informed decisions about what kinds of companies and roles you want to pursue.

### Professional identity development

Part of professional development is learning how to be a professional person, which is harder to develop in isolation than in community with other professionals.

## Advice for future remote interns

**If you're considering or starting a remote internship, here's what I wish I had known:**

### Be proactive about relationships

Don't wait for people to reach out to you. Schedule informal conversations, ask questions, and make an effort to get to know your colleagues as people, not just as professional contacts.

### Advocate for your learning goals

Be explicit about what you want to learn and regularly check in with your manager about whether you're getting the experiences you need for your professional development.

### Create boundaries and structure

Establish clear work hours, dedicated workspace, and routines that help you separate work from personal life.

### Seek multiple perspectives

Make an effort to talk to people in different roles and departments to get a more complete picture of the company and industry.

### Document your experience

Keep track of what you're learning, what challenges you're facing, and what you want to focus on next. This helps with both professional development and future job interviews.

### Consider the full cost

Factor in the potential learning and relationship deficits when evaluating remote opportunities. Sometimes a less prestigious in-person internship might be more valuable for your development.

  **Personal reflection:** Remote internships aren't inherently bad, but they require much more intentional effort from both interns and companies to be successful. The convenience comes with hidden costs that are easy to underestimate.
</Callout>

## The bigger picture

**My remote internship experience taught me that work is about much more than just completing tasks.**

It's about learning from other people, building relationships that support your career, understanding how organizations function, and developing as a professional person. These aspects of work are much harder to access remotely.

**That doesn't mean remote work is bad**—many experienced professionals thrive in remote environments. But as someone early in your career, the social and cultural aspects of work are particularly important for your development.

**For future internships, I'll be looking for opportunities that prioritize relationship building, mentorship, and cultural immersion, even if that means sacrificing some of the convenience of remote work.**
