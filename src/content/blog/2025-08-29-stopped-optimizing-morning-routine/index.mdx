## The morning routine that ruled my life

**For about two years, I was obsessed with having the "perfect" morning routine.**

I had read all the articles, watched all the YouTube videos, and listened to all the podcasts about how successful people start their days. The message was clear: if you want to be productive and successful, you need an optimized morning routine.

**My routine looked something like this:**
- 6:00 AM: Wake up (no snooze button, obviously)
- 6:05 AM: Drink a large glass of water
- 6:10 AM: 10 minutes of meditation
- 6:20 AM: Write three pages in a journal (morning pages)
- 6:50 AM: Review goals and plan the day
- 7:00 AM: Exercise (alternating between gym and home workouts)
- 8:00 AM: Protein-rich breakfast while reading something educational
- 8:30 AM: Shower and get ready
- 9:00 AM: Start work/classes feeling "optimized" and ready

**It looked great on paper.** It hit all the productivity guru checkboxes: early wake-up, mindfulness, journaling, goal-setting, exercise, learning, and proper nutrition.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:clock">
  **The problem:** This routine required 3 hours of my day, perfect conditions, and an unrealistic amount of willpower to maintain consistently. And when I inevitably failed to stick to it perfectly, I felt like I was failing at life.
</Callout>

---

## The cracks in the system

**At first, the routine felt amazing.** I was waking up early, feeling productive, and starting each day with a sense of accomplishment. I felt like I was finally "adulting" properly.

**But slowly, problems started to emerge:**

### The rigidity trap

If I woke up 20 minutes late, my entire day felt off. If I skipped one component, I felt like the whole routine was ruined. The routine became a master I served rather than a tool that served me.

### The guilt spiral

On days when I couldn't complete the full routine (which became increasingly frequent), I felt guilty and like I was "behind" before my day even started. This was the opposite of the positive, energized feeling the routine was supposed to create.

### The time pressure

Three hours is a lot of time, especially when you're a student with irregular schedules. Late nights studying meant early mornings were torture. Social events that ran late meant choosing between sleep and the routine.

### The one-size-fits-all fallacy

Some days I needed more sleep. Some days I was genuinely excited to jump into work immediately. Some days I wanted to have breakfast with friends instead of reading alone. The routine didn't account for natural variation in energy, mood, or circumstances.

### The optimization treadmill

I kept trying to optimize the routine further. Maybe I needed different meditation apps. Maybe I should wake up even earlier. Maybe I needed to track more metrics. The routine became an end in itself rather than a means to an end.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:lightbulb" classes="border-orange-950/100 bg-orange-100 dark:border-orange-100/100 dark:bg-orange-950">
  **The realization:** I was spending more mental energy managing my morning routine than I was gaining from it. The system designed to give me more energy was actually draining it.
</Callout>

---

## The breaking point

**The moment I realized this wasn't working came during finals week of my third year.**

I had been up until 2 AM studying for an exam, and my alarm went off at 6 AM as usual. I was exhausted, stressed about the exam, and the last thing I wanted to do was meditate and journal about my goals.

**But I tried to do the routine anyway** because I thought skipping it would make my day even worse.

I made it through about half of it before I realized I was just going through the motions in a zombie-like state. The meditation was just sitting with my eyes closed thinking about the exam. The journaling was mindless scribbling. The exercise was half-hearted and potentially unsafe given how tired I was.

**I was performing productivity theater instead of actually being productive.**

That's when it clicked: the routine had become a source of stress rather than a solution to it.

## The research that changed my mind

**Around the same time, I started reading more critically about morning routines and productivity advice.**

### The survivorship bias problem

Most morning routine advice comes from people who are naturally early risers, have flexible schedules, or have achieved success despite their routines rather than because of them. The advice is survivorship bias—you don't hear from the thousands of people for whom elaborate morning routines didn't work.

### The individual differences research

Studies on chronotypes (natural sleep-wake preferences) show that people have genuinely different optimal times for various activities. Some people are naturally most alert in the morning, others in the evening. Fighting against your natural chronotype often backfires.

### The habit formation science

Real habit formation research suggests that trying to establish multiple complex habits simultaneously has a very low success rate. The more components your routine has, the more likely you are to fail at maintaining it.

### The stress and performance research

Chronic sleep deprivation (often caused by forcing early wake-up times) has much more negative effects on cognitive performance than skipping morning meditation or journaling.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:brain">
  **The evidence:** Most of the specific components of elaborate morning routines don't have strong research support for universal benefits. The benefits are often individual and context-dependent.
</Callout>

## What I do instead now

**My current "morning routine" is embarrassingly simple:**

1. **Wake up when I wake up** (usually between 7-8 AM, sometimes later on weekends)
2. **Drink some water** (because I'm usually thirsty)
3. **Use the bathroom** (obviously)
4. **Check if there's anything urgent** (quick email/message scan)
5. **Get dressed**
6. **Eat something if I'm hungry**
7. **Start my day**

**That's it.** The entire "routine" takes maybe 20-30 minutes, and there's no guilt if it takes longer or if I do things in a different order.

### The flexibility principle

Instead of a rigid routine, I have a flexible set of options based on how I'm feeling and what my day looks like:

- **If I'm energized:** I might jump straight into work or head to the gym
- **If I'm groggy:** I might take a shower or go for a walk to wake up
- **If I'm anxious:** I might do some quick meditation or breathing exercises
- **If I'm excited about something:** I let myself dive into it immediately instead of forcing myself through routine steps

### The context-dependent approach

Different days call for different approaches:

- **Before important exams or meetings:** I might wake up a bit earlier to review material or mentally prepare
- **After late nights:** I prioritize sleep over early wake-up times
- **When I'm traveling or staying elsewhere:** I don't stress about maintaining any particular routine
- **During stressful periods:** I simplify even further and focus just on basic needs

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:target" classes="border-green-950/100 bg-green-100 dark:border-green-100/100 dark:bg-green-950">
  **The key insight:** Instead of optimizing for an ideal morning, I optimize for reducing friction between waking up and being productive. Most days, the best thing I can do is just get out of bed and start working on whatever matters most.
</Callout>

## What I learned about productivity advice

**This experience taught me to be much more skeptical of one-size-fits-all productivity advice.**

### The guru problem

Most productivity advice comes from people selling courses, books, or coaching services. Their incentive is to make their advice sound revolutionary and comprehensive, not necessarily to give you the simplest solution that works.

### The complexity bias

Humans have a bias toward thinking that complex solutions are better than simple ones. We assume that if something is worth doing, it should be elaborate and difficult. But often the opposite is true.

### The lifestyle privilege

Many elaborate routines require significant lifestyle privilege: flexible schedules, no long commutes, no young children, good health, financial stability. Advice that ignores these realities isn't helpful for most people.

### The optimization trap

There's a difference between optimizing for short-term performance and optimizing for long-term sustainability. Elaborate routines often sacrifice sustainability for the promise of peak performance.

## The benefits of my simpler approach

### Less decision fatigue

Not having to decide whether to stick to my routine, modify it, or skip it entirely saves mental energy for more important decisions throughout the day.

### Better sleep quality

Not having to wake up at a specific early time means I can get the sleep I actually need, which has much bigger benefits for my energy and mood than any morning routine components.

### Reduced guilt and self-criticism

When your morning routine is "wake up and start your day," it's pretty hard to fail at it. This eliminates a major source of daily self-criticism and guilt.

### More responsiveness to natural rhythms

Some days I naturally wake up energized and ready to work. Some days I need a slower start. Allowing for this variation feels more sustainable and human.

### Time savings

Getting back 1-2 hours of my morning means more time for sleep, more time for actual work, or more time for spontaneous activities and social connections.

### Lower stress

Not having to perform a complex routine first thing in the morning means I can respond to urgent situations, changing schedules, or just different moods without feeling like I'm failing at self-care.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:smile">
  **Surprising result:** My overall productivity and wellbeing improved when I simplified my morning routine. I had more energy for the work that actually mattered and less stress about optimizing my optimization systems.
</Callout>

## What I kept from my old routine

**I didn't throw everything away—some components were genuinely helpful, just not as part of a rigid morning system:**

### Meditation (but flexible)

I still meditate regularly, but when it feels right rather than at a prescribed time. Sometimes that's in the morning, sometimes in the evening, sometimes in the middle of a stressful day.

### Exercise (but sustainable)

I still exercise regularly, but I work out when it fits my schedule and energy levels rather than forcing it into every morning. Some weeks that's morning gym sessions, some weeks it's evening walks.

### Planning (but lightweight)

I still plan my days, but usually while I'm walking to my first class or during a natural transition moment, not as part of an elaborate morning ritual.

### Hydration (but obvious)

I still drink water in the morning because I'm usually thirsty when I wake up. No need to make this a conscious optimization.

### Journaling (but occasional)

I still write for self-reflection, but only when I have something to work through rather than as a daily obligation that often resulted in empty pages.

## When morning routines do make sense

**I don't want to dismiss morning routines entirely—they can be valuable for some people in some contexts:**

### For natural early risers

If you genuinely feel best when you wake up early and have energy for multiple activities before work, a structured routine might help you make the most of that natural energy.

### During stable life periods

When your schedule is predictable and you don't have major stressors or changes, maintaining a routine can provide helpful structure.

### For specific goals

If you're training for something specific (like a marathon) or working toward a particular goal (like learning a language), incorporating that into a morning routine might be effective.

### As temporary structure

During periods when you need more structure (like recovering from depression or major life changes), having a routine can provide helpful scaffolding.

### When you genuinely enjoy it

If you actually like your morning routine and it brings you joy rather than stress, there's no reason to change it.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:compass" classes="border-blue-950/100 bg-blue-100 dark:border-blue-100/100 dark:bg-blue-950">
  **The key question:** Is your morning routine serving you, or are you serving it? If it's creating more stress than benefit, it might be time to simplify.
</Callout>

## My advice for morning routine optimization

**If you're thinking about creating or optimizing a morning routine, here's what I wish I had known:**

### Start with one thing

Don't try to build a comprehensive routine all at once. Pick one morning habit that would genuinely improve your day and focus on that until it's automatic.

### Question the "shoulds"

Just because successful people do something in the morning doesn't mean you should. Question whether each component actually benefits you personally.

### Optimize for sustainability, not peak performance

A simple routine you can maintain for years is better than an elaborate routine you abandon after a few months.

### Build in flexibility

Design your routine to accommodate different energy levels, schedules, and life circumstances. Rigid systems break under real-world pressures.

### Focus on sleep first

Getting enough quality sleep will do more for your morning energy and overall productivity than any routine component.

### Measure outcomes, not inputs

Judge your morning routine by how you feel throughout the day and how productive you are overall, not by how perfectly you executed the routine itself.

## The broader lesson about self-improvement

**This experience taught me something important about self-improvement in general:**

### More isn't always better

The most effective interventions are often the simplest ones. Adding complexity doesn't necessarily add value.

### Individual differences matter

What works for one person might not work for another. Be willing to experiment and find what actually works for your personality, schedule, and goals.

### Sustainability beats optimization

A "good enough" system that you can maintain indefinitely is better than a "perfect" system that you abandon after a few weeks.

### Systems should serve goals, not become goals

It's easy for productivity systems to become ends in themselves rather than means to accomplish what you actually care about.

### Sometimes the best improvement is subtraction

Instead of asking "What should I add to make this better?" sometimes ask "What can I remove to make this simpler?"

## The current reality

**So how do I actually start my mornings now?**

**Most days, I wake up around 7:30 AM** (sometimes earlier if I'm excited about something, sometimes later if I stayed up late). I check my phone to see if there's anything urgent, get up, use the bathroom, drink some water, and figure out what needs to happen that day.

**If I'm hungry, I eat something.** If I need to be somewhere, I get ready. If I want to exercise, I do that. If I have energy for work, I dive in immediately.

**There's no guilt, no complex decision-making, and no performance anxiety.** Just a human waking up and responding to their needs and circumstances.

**And you know what? My days are better.** I have more energy, less stress, and more time for things that actually matter. Sometimes the best optimization is no optimization at all.
