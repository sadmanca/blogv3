## The realization that hit too late

**I'm in my fourth year at the University of Toronto now, and I've been doing a lot of reflecting lately about how I've spent the past three and a half years.**

The academics have been fine. I've done well in my courses, learned a lot about computer science and engineering, built some cool projects, and even managed to land some decent internships along the way.

**But if I'm being honest with myself, I have one major regret: I didn't prioritize my social life nearly enough.**

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:clock">
  **The thing about university:** You get exactly four (or five, if you're doing a longer program) years of this unique environment where you're surrounded by thousands of smart, interesting people your age who are all figuring out life together. Once it's over, you never get that back.
</Callout>

---

## How I got here

**Looking back, I can see exactly how this happened.**

I came into university with a very specific mindset: I was here to get good grades, build technical skills, and set myself up for a successful career. Everything else felt secondary.

**First year:** I was focused on adjusting to the academic workload and figuring out the basics of living away from home. I told myself I'd have more time for social stuff once I got settled.

**Second year:** The courses got harder, I started getting more serious about internships and research opportunities. I told myself I'd make more friends once I had my career path figured out.

**Third year:** I was deep into the internship application grind, working on side projects, and trying to maintain my GPA. I told myself I'd focus on relationships more once the job search pressure eased up.

**Fourth year:** And here I am, realizing that "once I get this figured out" never actually comes. There's always another goal, another milestone, another thing to optimize for.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:lightbulb" classes="border-orange-950/100 bg-orange-100 dark:border-orange-100/100 dark:bg-orange-950">
  **The pattern I now recognize:** I kept treating social connections as something I'd "get to later" instead of recognizing them as an integral part of the university experience that deserved intentional investment.
</Callout>

---

## What I missed out on

**It's not like I had zero social life—I made some friends, had some good times, and I don't want to make it sound like I was a complete hermit.**

But I definitely missed opportunities for deeper connections and experiences that I'll probably never get again.

### The friendships that could have been

There were so many people I met in classes, labs, or random campus events where we'd have great conversations, exchange contacts, and say "we should hang out sometime"—and then never follow up because I was always busy with assignments or focused on the next academic deadline.

**How many potential close friendships did I let slip away because I prioritized problem sets over people?**

### The spontaneous experiences

University is full of random opportunities: last-minute concerts, impromptu study abroad trips, weird club events, late-night adventures around the city. I said no to most of these because they didn't fit into my carefully planned schedule.

### The social learning

Some of the most important life skills—communication, collaboration, understanding different perspectives, navigating group dynamics—are best learned through social interaction. You can't really get that from textbooks or coding projects.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:heart">
  **The honest truth:** I optimized for individual achievement at the expense of interpersonal connection. And while the achievement was valuable, I'm starting to realize that the connections might have been more important in the long run.
</Callout>

---

## Why this matters more than I thought

**I used to think that focusing on academics and career prep was the "responsible" thing to do, and that social stuff was just... nice to have.**

But as I've gotten older and started thinking about what I actually want from life, I'm realizing how backwards that thinking was.

### Professional networks are social networks

Almost every interesting opportunity I've gotten—internships, research positions, project collaborations—has come through people I know, not through cold applications. The students who invested more in relationships often had more diverse and interesting career paths, not because they were less capable, but because they had access to more information and opportunities.

### Mental health and fulfillment

The times when I've felt most stressed, burned out, or questioning my path have usually been when I was most isolated. Having people to talk through problems with, to celebrate successes with, to just hang out with when you need a break—that's not luxury, that's essential for wellbeing.

### Life skills you can't learn alone

Working in teams, reading social situations, understanding different communication styles, managing conflict, supporting friends through difficult times—these are all skills that matter enormously in both personal and professional contexts.

### The memories that actually matter

When I think about my most meaningful university experiences, they're almost all social: late-night conversations with roommates, group projects that actually clicked, random adventures around Toronto, celebrating big milestones with friends.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:graduation-cap" classes="border-blue-950/100 bg-blue-100 dark:border-blue-100/100 dark:bg-blue-950">
  **Perspective shift:** The academic achievements I was so focused on are mostly forgotten details now. But the relationships and shared experiences? Those are the things that actually shaped who I am and how I think about the world.
</Callout>

---

## The University of Toronto effect

**I think this problem is particularly acute at U of T, and probably at other highly competitive universities.**

The academic pressure is intense, the student body is incredibly ambitious, and there's this underlying current of competition that makes it easy to get tunnel vision about grades and career outcomes.

**Everyone's optimizing for the same things:** high GPA, prestigious internships, research publications, graduate school admissions. It's easy to get caught up in that race and forget that university is also supposed to be about personal growth, exploration, and building relationships.

The campus culture doesn't always encourage deep social connection either. With such a large student body spread across multiple campuses, it takes more intentional effort to build community than it might at smaller schools.

**But here's the thing:** The students who seemed happiest and most well-rounded were usually the ones who found ways to balance academic achievement with genuine social investment.

---

## What I'm doing about it (fourth year plans)

**I can't go back and change how I spent my first three years, but I still have two semesters left, and I'm determined to make them count socially.**

### Being more intentional about existing friendships

Instead of just seeing people in class or study groups, I'm actually following up on those "we should hang out" conversations. Suggesting specific plans, initiating group activities, making social connection a priority instead of an afterthought.

### Saying yes more often

My default has been to say no to social invitations if I have work to do. I'm trying to flip that—now my default is yes unless there's a genuinely compelling reason to decline.

### Joining things for the social aspect

I've mostly chosen extracurriculars based on resume value or learning opportunities. This year I'm joining some things just because they seem fun and like good ways to meet people with shared interests.

### Making an effort with acquaintances

There are so many people I've had pleasant interactions with over the years but never developed deeper friendships with. I'm trying to be more proactive about turning those surface-level connections into real relationships.

### Exploring Toronto more

I've lived in this city for almost four years and there are still huge parts of it I've never explored. Planning more social activities that get me off campus and into the broader Toronto community.

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:calendar">
  **Concrete commitment:** I'm scheduling at least one purely social activity per week for the rest of the academic year. No academic justification required—just fun and connection for their own sake.
</Callout>

## Advice for current students

**If you're reading this and you're still early in your university experience, here's what I wish someone had told me:**

### Relationships are not a distraction from your goals—they're part of your goals

The people you meet in university can become lifelong friends, professional collaborators, creative partners, and sources of support through all kinds of life changes. Investing time in those relationships isn't taking away from your future—it's building it.

### Academic success and social life aren't mutually exclusive

Some of my most productive study sessions happened in groups. Some of my best creative ideas came from conversations with friends. Some of my most effective stress management came from having people to talk things through with.

### Quality over quantity, but don't neglect quantity either

You don't need to be friends with everyone, but you do need to meet enough people to find the ones you genuinely connect with. That requires putting yourself in social situations regularly, not just occasionally.

### Take advantage of structured social opportunities

Join clubs, participate in residence activities, attend campus events, sign up for group projects. These provide natural contexts for meeting people without the awkwardness of having to initiate everything yourself.

### Follow up on connections

When you meet someone interesting, actually follow up. Send that text, suggest that coffee date, invite them to study together. Most people are looking for connection too—they're just waiting for someone else to make the first move.

<Callout type="warning" icon="lucide:users" classes="border-red-950/100 bg-red-100 dark:border-red-100/100 dark:bg-red-950">
  **The university advantage:** Never again in your life will you be in an environment with so many interesting people your age who are all actively looking to make new friends and try new things. Don't waste that opportunity.
</Callout>

## The broader life lesson

**This isn't really just about university—it's about how I prioritize things in general.**

I have a tendency to treat relationships as something I'll "get to" once I have other areas of my life figured out. But relationships aren't a reward for achieving other goals—they're part of what makes those other goals meaningful in the first place.

**Career success without people to share it with feels empty.**
**Personal achievements without friends to celebrate with lose their significance.**
**Learning and growth without diverse perspectives to challenge and expand your thinking becomes limiting.**

### The optimization trap

As someone who likes to optimize and systematize things, it's easy to fall into the trap of treating social connection as inefficient. It takes time, it's unpredictable, it doesn't always have clear ROI.

But some of the most important things in life can't be optimized. They require presence, spontaneity, and genuine investment without guaranteed returns.

---

## Making peace with regret

**I don't want this post to sound like I'm beating myself up or dwelling in regret.**

The choices I made were understandable given my priorities and mindset at the time. I was trying to do well academically and set myself up for career success, and I did achieve those things.

**But I've learned that life isn't just about achieving goals—it's about who you become and who you share the journey with along the way.**

<Callout type="info" icon="lucide:compass" classes="border-green-950/100 bg-green-100 dark:border-green-100/100 dark:bg-green-950">
  **The silver lining:** Recognizing this pattern now, in fourth year, is better than recognizing it after graduation when it's too late to do anything about it. I still have time to course-correct and make the most of my remaining university experience.
</Callout>

---

## To my future self

**When I'm 30, or 40, or looking back on my university years from whatever vantage point life gives me, here's what I hope I can say:**

That I learned to balance achievement with connection. That I took risks on relationships even when the outcomes were uncertain. That I showed up for people and let people show up for me. That I created memories worth remembering and friendships worth maintaining.

**That I figured out how to be successful without being isolated.**

And if there are younger students reading this who are making the same mistakes I made—prioritizing grades over people, optimization over experience, individual achievement over community—I hope this gives you permission to recalibrate while there's still time.

**University is short. The friendships you make there can last a lifetime. Don't optimize yourself out of the best parts of the experience.**
